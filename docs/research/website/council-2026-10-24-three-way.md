# Pressure test, three ways: Claude council, ChatGPT and Gemini

On 2026-10-24 the same blind pressure test was run three times: by a Claude council (five advisors, peer review, chairman; transcript in `council-2026-10-24.md`), by ChatGPT and by Gemini. ChatGPT and Gemini got the same question, the same option descriptions and the same contrast facts as one text file, plus three images showing options A to O, in the owner's Chrome. Both answers are copied below word for word. In Gemini's answer one em dash was replaced with a colon, and its "TXT" source markers were removed.

Key to the letters, never shown to any judge: A Editorial Signal (ChatGPT), B Architectural Blueprint (Gemini), C The Chameleon (Gemini), D Adaptive Prism (ChatGPT), E The Translator (Gemini), F Modular Architecture (ChatGPT), G Living Material (ChatGPT), H Soft Contrast (Gemini), I Plain sheet, J Neel, K Kolam, L Two brands, M Bare Bones (Gemini), N Spec sheet, O Script & Signal (ChatGPT).

## What the three agree on

- J, N and L are the three serious directions. L is third for all three judges, and every judge uses it as a demonstration inside another look, never as the look itself.
- J has the best palette and the cleanest contrast record.
- D, H, O and B are near the bottom for all three.
- None of the three judges ranked its own tool's directions highly. ChatGPT placed its own five at 5, 6, 9, 12 and 15. Gemini placed its own five at 8, 10, 11, 14 and 15.

## Where they differ

- **Lead.** Two of three lead with J's look; the Claude council leads with N's structure.
- **The serif.** Two of three keep it; the Claude council drops it because it exists only in Latin.
- **Plain sheet.** Gemini ranks it second for its public-service clarity; the others place it fourth because it looks like an existing government system.

## ChatGPT's answer

1. One-line verdict on each option

A: Editorial Signal. A credible, content-first direction with the right restraint, but its orange contrast failures and familiar documentation aesthetic need fixing.

B: Architectural Blueprint. Strong conceptual coherence and engineering character, but the drafting metaphor is too literal, and its contrast failures undermine a system built around reliable implementation.

C: The Chameleon. The clearest demonstration of themeability, with excellent reported contrast results, but making the documentation itself support multiple themes risks turning the showcase into an expensive maintenance burden.

D: Adaptive Prism. Immediately communicates adaptability, but the gradients, translucent layers, multiple accent hues and weak contrast pairs conflict with Saral's constraints.

E: The Translator. A strong commitment to multilingual typography and technical clarity, but neon green, compressed layouts and a terminal-like aesthetic make the overall experience unnecessarily severe.

F: Modular Architecture. A convincing developer-facing system with a practical component composition model, but its multicolour palette violates the single-accent constraint and its failed contrast pairs need correction.

G: Living Material. The most approachable expression of technical infrastructure, but the earthy palette and tactile surfaces risk making Saral look like a sustainability brand rather than a dependable, universal design system.

H: Soft Contrast. A humane public-service direction with sound accessibility instincts, but its weak measured contrast and subdued visual identity make it a poor foundation without substantial revision.

I: Plain Sheet. The strongest immediately usable public-service direction, with excellent reported contrast results and no-nonsense content, but its resemblance to established government systems limits its distinctiveness.

J: Neel. A mature, coherent direction with excellent reported contrast results, credible documentation structure and a restrained visual identity, although its indigo palette and editorial serif could feel conventional.

K: Kolam. A potentially distinctive, culturally rooted visual language, but the literal motif, pill controls and ornamental emphasis risk narrowing Saral's global identity, while its contrast failures need correction.

L: Two Brands. The strongest proof of the product's central promise: identical components can serve very different brand identities, but the brands risk becoming the protagonists instead of Saral.

M: Bare Bones. The most direct expression of native HTML and progressive enhancement, but the intentionally raw aesthetic could be mistaken for an unfinished product, and the contrast failures are avoidable.

N: Spec Sheet. The strongest expression of engineering accountability, with concrete performance and implementation evidence, but its density and coldness risk excluding designers and less technical decision-makers.

O: Script & Signal. A memorable graphic signature that can make a restrained technical product recognisable, but its multicolour palette breaks the accent constraint and its expressive styling could overwhelm the documentation.

2. Top three, ranked

1. J: Neel. The best overall foundation. It combines a disciplined visual identity, readable editorial hierarchy, practical form examples, complete component documentation structure and the strongest reported contrast result, with all 9 of 9 tested pairs passing. Its restrained, single-hue palette is compatible with the one-accent rule. It looks like a system people can trust and use for years, rather than a design trend.

2. N: Spec Sheet. The strongest product-specific direction. It makes Saral's actual advantages visible: native HTML, zero runtime dependencies, performance, progressive enhancement and machine-readable specifications. The interface earns credibility through evidence rather than claims. Its eight passing pairs out of nine are a good starting point, though the dark orange needs correction and the dense presentation needs a deliberate hierarchy for non-engineers.

3. L: Two Brands. The strongest strategic demonstration. Showing one component in two unrelated brand themes proves themeability more convincingly than an abstract palette switch. The contrast between the themes also gives the otherwise neutral framework a memorable purpose. It ranks third because it demonstrates what Saral enables more effectively than it establishes Saral's own enduring identity.

3. The style I would choose

Neel's visual foundation, Spec Sheet's information architecture and evidence, and Two Brands' live theme comparison.

Use J's restrained indigo-and-white palette, editorial hierarchy, quiet rules and carefully controlled spacing. Structure every component page around N's concrete implementation facts, accessible usage guidance, native HTML, API, tokens, states and performance evidence. Bring in L's two-brand demonstration as a recurring proof point, showing that the same semantic component works across two genuinely different themes. Keep the surrounding interface neutral, with one accent hue, and reserve the brand-specific treatments for the live examples. The result should feel precise, calm and technically serious, with a distinctive identity coming from its structure and demonstrations rather than decorative effects.

4. The biggest risk

Choosing a polished concept that cannot survive implementation.

The brief imposes stricter constraints than most of the visual directions demonstrate. A successful mock-up does not prove that the site can run entirely on Saral's own tokens and components, remain useful without JavaScript, work in Windows forced colours, load within 150 kB including fonts, and render correctly in Latin, Devanagari, Tamil, Arabic, Thai and Japanese.

J's contrast score is promising, not a complete accessibility certification. Its type choices and script coverage still need verification, as do dark-theme behaviour and actual component states. N's performance-first philosophy helps, but its dense layout needs testing at small viewports and with translated content. L's paired themes must remain genuine theme configurations, not separate implementations that quietly bypass the system.

Before committing to the direction, build one real component page and one real form in the proposed theme. Test them with JavaScript disabled, forced colours, keyboard navigation, screen readers, all six required scripts, light and dark themes, and a measured production build. If the visual concept cannot pass those tests, it is not the right concept, however good the presentation looks.

## Gemini's answer

1. One-line verdicts on each option

Option A: Clean and content-first, but relies too heavily on an accent color that currently fails accessibility standards.

Option B: A rigid, overly literal drafting metaphor that risks alienating consumer-facing product teams.

Option C: Effectively demonstrates token flexibility, but introduces prohibitive maintenance complexity for the documentation site itself.

Option D: Trendy translucent layering creates visual noise and undermines the system's core promise of stable, inclusive infrastructure.

Option E: A stark, terminal-inspired aesthetic that prioritizes linguistic fluidity but feels too academic for broad adoption.

Option F: Highly logical and modular, yet risks feeling cold and detached without distinct product contexts.

Option G: Brings a welcome tactile warmth suitable for public services, provided the severe contrast failures in its earth tones are corrected.

Option H: Aims for trust and calm but fails fundamental contrast requirements, defeating its own accessibility-first premise.

Option I: Extremely functional and highly compliant, though it leans so far into utility that it closely resembles an existing government portal.

Option J: Authoritative, editorial, and flawlessly accessible, striking the ideal balance of trust and polish for financial and civic sectors.

Option K: Introduces a compelling cultural signature, but borders on literal ornamentation which violates the founder's foundational constraints.

Option L: Powerfully proves the system's agnostic nature, but sacrifices the system's own distinct identity to the demo brands.

Option M: An uncompromising brutalist approach that champions raw performance but will read as unfinished to non-technical stakeholders.

Option N: A dense, highly technical spec sheet that thrills engineers but lacks the approachability required for product designers.

Option O: Visually striking and memorable, but the expressive graphic marks risk feeling too chaotic for serious civic or financial tools.

2. Top three options, ranked

Option J: It is the only visual direction with a flawless contrast pass rate (9 of 9), immediately satisfying the strict WCAG 2.2 AA constraint while projecting the exact trust, calm, and editorial polish required for global financial and public service products.

Option I: It directly answers the brief's mandate for plain words and performant native HTML elements, offering robust contrast compliance (8 of 9) and an unmistakable, distraction-free public-service utility.

Option L: It acts as a perfect mechanical proof of the system's core promise: themeable tokens styling identical HTML elements, while maintaining an exceptional 10 of 11 contrast pass rate.

3. The chosen style

Neel's Foundation with L's Proof: This direction uses Option J's fully accessible, deep indigo color palette, editorial spacing, and refined typography (Newsreader and Anek Latin) to establish a trusted, world-class baseline. However, it replaces J's static theme row with Option L's "Two Brands" interactive toggle structure on the homepage, allowing users to instantly contrast two highly distinct visual themes applied to the exact same underlying markup. This creates an elegant, highly accessible documentation shell that actively proves its technical flexibility without sacrificing its own premium, trustworthy identity.

4. The biggest risk in the choice

The biggest risk in this combined approach is that the deep indigo and serif-driven elegance of the Neel baseline might subconsciously anchor developers into viewing Saral strictly as a high-end editorial or financial system, potentially obscuring its capability to power highly playful or hyper-minimalist consumer brands if the "Two Brands" demonstration themes are not extreme enough in their visual contrast.
